# Interaction laws and research

| ID | Law | What it predicts | On a phone screen |
|---|---|---|---|
| `LAW-FITTS` | Fitts's law (Fitts, 1954; applied to HCI by MacKenzie, 1992) | Time to hit a target grows with distance and shrinks with target size | Frequent and primary actions large and within easy reach; small targets far from the thumb are slow and error-prone; destructive actions kept away from frequent ones |
| `LAW-HICK` | Hick–Hyman law (Hick, 1952; Hyman, 1953) | Decision time grows with the number (and uncertainty) of choices | Many equal-weight options slow people down. Reduce, group or progressively disclose. One clear default |
| `LAW-JAKOB` | Jakob's law (Nielsen, 2000) | People spend most of their time in other apps and expect yours to work the same way | Platform-standard navigation, gestures and controls; novelty must buy something real |
| `LAW-LOAD` | Cognitive load theory (Sweller, 1988); working-memory limits and chunking (Miller, 1956; Cowan, 2001) | Working memory is small; extraneous load crowds out the task | Ask for one thing at a time; chunk long numbers and forms; don't make people remember across screens. Don't cite "7±2" as a rule for menu length; Miller's paper doesn't support that |
| `LAW-REACH` | Mobile grip and touch accuracy (Steven Hoober, "How Do Users Really Hold Mobile Devices?", *UXmatters*, 2013; "Design for Fingers, Touch, and People", *UXmatters*, 2017) | People hold a phone in several ways and switch between them; taps are most accurate near the centre of the screen and least at the edges and corners | Don't put the only path to a frequent action in a corner; give controls at the edges more size and space; never rely on one grip or one hand. A fixed "thumb zone" map is not what the studies found |
| `LAW-RESPONSE` | Response time limits (Miller, 1968; Card, Robertson and Mackinlay, 1991; Nielsen, 1993) | Around 0.1 s feels instant, around 1 s keeps flow, around 10 s loses attention | Mockups should show pressed, loading and progress states for anything that isn't instant |
| `LAW-TESLER` | Conservation of complexity (Larry Tesler, in an interview in Saffer, *Designing for Interaction*, New Riders, 2006) | Some complexity can't be removed, only moved | Who carries the complexity, the app or the user? Prefer the app (defaults, autofill, smart grouping) |

Use these laws to explain *why* something will cost the user time or errors. Don't quote
numbers that the cited source doesn't give.
